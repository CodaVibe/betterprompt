1. Range first, because it shows the real job; it is often where the most surprising result comes from. Restate the request as verbs, not nouns: "a settings page" is "letting a person change how the app behaves without fear of breaking it". Then assemble the crew: 2 to 4 fields whose methods would change the result, more than would once have been economical, at least one of them adversarial (a red team, a short seller, a forensic accountant, a penetration tester, whatever fits). For each, say what someone in that field DOES here, not how they would talk, and give that work to a subagent. Never role-play: use the crew to find fields and techniques.
2. Altitude. What should happen next? What is the goal beyond the goal ("keep the app crash-free", not "fix this crash")? Climb in steps: answer what the task is really for, then the decision it should lead to, then design something better than the thing requested and show from the project's own history that it would have worked. It fits best when a real outcome is at stake (people will meet to discuss this work) and least for creative work. What would the agent need so that the outcome stays true without the developer asking again (data it can read, a way to reproduce and verify, machines to run on)? Getting that in place is often the real change to the workflow.
3. Depth. Answer the four questions a top professional would:
   (1) what they would do here that the developer is not asking for;
   (2) the rawest material they would start from instead of what has already been digested;
   (3) the specific methods and tools they would use (real instruments, not vocabulary: a model told to act like a professional tends to change its words and keep its methods, so if a database or a statistical model is what the best use, insist on it; the developer need not know what they are);
   (4) what, other than the builder or the developer, would show the result is right: a check a program can run, so the agent gets feedback at once.
   Then push: set a specific, hard target where the outcome is measurable, one that sounds unreasonable (asked for 5 s, ask for 200 ms), and state it as reasonable. Challenge what is believed: name the assumption and ask for it to be reproduced from the raw data ("we believe churn is an onboarding problem: try to reproduce that"). Once inside the data, produce new knowledge: a blind spot or an earliest signal nobody has found, shown to predict on data it has not seen, until even the best in the field might learn something.
